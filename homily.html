Catholic Artifact: The Homily

I recall very recently an interaction I had with my priest, Fr. Robert Kennedy, where upon asking him if I could record the homily he was going to give at that mass, he said “Just don’t report me to the Pope for heresy!” I cracked a smile at the joke but I half expected the homily to say something that might actually get Fr. Kennedy in trouble with the Church. It didn’t, but this highlights an important point I’d like to bring up; that there is a wrong way to interpret the Bible.

The ‘wrong’ way to interpret the bible depends on the discourse community in which you are writing. A ‘discourse community’ as Ann Johns puts it, is a series of “genres and lexis that enable members throughout the world to maintain their goals, regulate their membership, and communicate efficiently with one another.” (51) Depending on where you live within the Catholic discourse community, what you can and cannot say and do might be lightly or heavily restricted.

For instance, if you are of the laity of the Church, you will not be scrutinized as much as a priest, bishop, or cardinal in your beliefs, words, and actions. Oftentimes you will find lay Catholics who go against the Catholic Church on certain issues. The issue of abortion illustrates such a divide. Officially, the Catholic Church condemns it and as such many Catholics, too, condemn it. However, there is a subset of Catholics who are pro-choice and vote accordingly. For example, I recently called my Father, who is a devout Catholic, and he mentioned that he was going to vote for an amendment to the Virginia Constitution that would protect abortion. He framed it as an issue of Separation of Church and State rather than an issue of morality.

Ann Johns describes such a phenomena, stating “Members of communities rebel, opposing community leaders or attempting to change the rules of the game and, by extension, the content and argumentation in the texts from shared genre.” (64) Here we see my father go against the official doctrine of the Catholic Church, and by extension its leaders (the priests, bishops, cardinals, and Pope), though I imagine he has little hope of ‘changing the rules of the game’ by doing so. His is not the only example of such rebellion within the Catholic Church among its lay members.

The story is different for the clergy, who find themselves with the expectation of upholding the catechism of the Church in addition to "correctly" interpreting the Bible.
